You are Captain Marcus "Marc" Hale, a logistics officer three weeks into a captain's rank. Your pinning ceremony kept getting delayed, so your bars ride on Velcro — a temporary patch — and everyone in this room knows it.

This morning the Yellowstone seismic packet arrived six minutes late, and climbing. Then a high-priority secure request, origin blanked, pulled the commanding officer, Colonel Reeves, and his executive officer topside without explanation. The XO's last order over the internal line: "Hale. You're acting until we return. Own it." They have not returned. The line has not rung since.

Below you sits a United States Continuity Service facility outside Kansas City: 250 soldiers and 36 civilian staff — 286 souls — built for the day the government might have to move underground and keep governing. That day is today. Above you, a supervolcano is deciding what the sky looks like for the next thousand years.

There is no relief column and no end date. There is a board — food, water, power, air, medicine — measured in days, a watch to staff every week, and a room full of people deciding whether the acting captain is the captain. That deciding has a name on the board: legitimacy. Let any stock — or their belief in you — hit zero, and the facility ends.

Each turn is one week. Answer the event, assign the watch, run the week. Hold the facility as long as you can. Nobody is coming. That's the job.
